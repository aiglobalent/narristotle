The Method

The mind behind the method.

Narristotle is the editorial voice of a working methodology: the set of principles Matt Allen developed for building stories that hold together from the first spark to the final draft. It is also the thinking that guides RoughDraft Maker, the draft-building tool from AI Global Entertainment, where the technology and product development are led by Steve Smythe.

Four principles do most of the work. None of them is a formula. All of them are questions you can ask of any scene, at any stage, including the messy middle where most stories quietly give up.

I. Story Structure

From idea to a path forward. Structure is not a cage; it is a sequence of promises about where the reader is being taken. For screen stories, the method works from a beat-by-beat blueprint; for novels, from a chapter spine. Either way, the test is the same: at every point, does the reader know what they are hoping for, fearing, or waiting to find out? If the answer is nothing, the structure has a hole, and no amount of beautiful prose will paper over it for long.

II. Character Transformation

Give change a reason. Characters do not change because the plot needs a third act. They change because a choice costs them something they were protecting — a belief, a comfort, a version of themselves. The method tracks who the character is at the start, what pressure is applied, and which crucible choice proves the change is real. If your protagonist could learn the lesson in a pamphlet, the story has not made them earn it.

III. Setups & Payoffs

Plant early. Pay off later. Every story makes promises: an object shown, a skill mentioned, a wound described, a rule of the world. The method keeps track of those promises — the physical props and the emotional ones — across the whole work, so that payoffs arrive where they should and nothing the reader was asked to remember is dropped. A tracked setup is a courtesy to the reader. A forgotten one is a small betrayal.

IV. Dramatic Cause & Effect

Choices create momentum. Weak stories wander from event to event connected by “and then.” Strong ones move by “therefore” and “but”: this happened, therefore she did that, but it cost more than she expected. The method builds chains of consequence, so scenes are not episodes — they are dominoes the characters themselves push over.

THE METHOD IN PRACTICE — A DEMONSTRATION

BEFORE:

“She finds a key. Later, she escapes.”

AFTER:

“She keeps the key instead of selling it. When the door locks behind her, that choice becomes her way out.”

An illustrative example, written for this page, of setup, payoff, and consequence. In the “before,” the key is a coincidence waiting to happen. In the “after,” it is a choice — she gave up money to keep it — so the escape pays off her judgment, not the author’s luck.
