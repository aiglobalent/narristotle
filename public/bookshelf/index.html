Poetics

Aristotle

Title page of S. H. Butcher’s edition (Macmillan, 1902). Public domain, via Internet Archive.

Why study it: The oldest surviving serious attempt to explain why stories work, written by the philosopher whose name Narristotle cheerfully misappropriates. Beneath the ancient vocabulary is a practical argument: a plot is a whole made of parts in an order, and the order is the art.

Technique on display: Unity of action. Everything in the story belongs because it is necessary or probable — an ancestor of the setup-and-payoff tracking the Method still uses today.

Your takeaway: For each scene in your draft, ask the Poetics question: if I removed this, would anything later break? If nothing breaks, the scene is decoration. Either give it a consequence or enjoy cutting it.

Pride and Prejudice

Jane Austen

The 1894 “Peacock Edition” (George Allen), illustrated by Hugh Thomson. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Why study it: The finest demonstration in English of character change that the reader believes. Elizabeth Bennet does not transform because the plot requires a wedding; she transforms because evidence keeps arriving that her judgment — the thing she is proudest of — has been wrong in both directions.

Technique on display: Transformation through misjudgment and discovery. Every major turn is a piece of evidence reinterpreted, so the change is argued, scene by scene, rather than announced.

Your takeaway: Give your protagonist a confident, wrong belief early. Then let each act hand them one fact that belief cannot survive. Change that comes from evidence feels inevitable; change that comes from a speech feels rented.

Great Expectations

Charles Dickens

Marcus Stone’s plate for the 1862 illustrated edition: Pip before Miss Havisham. Public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.

Why study it: A masterclass in long-range setups. Objects, encounters, and kindnesses from the opening chapters return hundreds of pages later carrying entirely new meaning, and a revelation about Pip’s fortune rearranges everything the reader thought the story’s moral arithmetic was.

Technique on display: Revelation that reshapes the past. The late turn works because its evidence was planted in plain sight — the model of a fair twist the Gazette keeps prescribing.

Your takeaway: Keep a payoff ledger. List what your opening chapters plant — every object, favor, and unexplained kindness — and decide, in writing, where each one returns. If it never returns, it is not a plant. It is litter.

A Tomato, a Donkey, and the Stars

Steve Smythe · A Working Draft

Pip is a giant tomato who longs to do something important. Bramble is a donkey who likes to think before she takes a step. When they find a space capsule buried on their farm, Pip is ready for the biggest adventure ever. But inside is a tiny robot with a broken ship—and a very long way to go home. With farm tools, bright ideas, and a few muddy mistakes, the friends set out to help. Pip soon discovers that being big does not mean doing everything yourself. Sometimes the most important thing you can do is stop, listen, and trust a friend.

A working draft, published exactly as it came off the desk — the adventure is complete, the chapters are lean, and the second pass is still to come.

The House of Second Chances

Narristotle · A Working Draft

Mara returns to her river town to sell the old hotel she inherited, certain her late father ruined the family by refusing a lucrative development deal. When a burst pipe reveals a hidden room of letters and keepsakes, each discovery changes what Mara believes about her father—and about the deal she is about to sign. As a flood threatens the town and the developer’s deadline approaches, Mara must decide whether to preserve the hotel or let it go.

This is not a finished novel, and it doesn’t pretend to be. It’s a working draft, published exactly as RoughDraft Maker produced it — the story is complete, beginning to end. The rewriting, the part that makes it a writer’s own, is still to come.
