Tonight, Let the Monster Want Something.

Halloween hands writers a gift and a trap in the same paper bag: atmosphere is free, but atmosphere is not a story.

THE SHORT ANSWER

The scariest Halloween writing advice is also the simplest: a monster that only frightens is a costume, but a monster that wants something is a character. Give tonight's visitor a want, an obstacle, and one honest sentence, and the scene writes itself.

Halloween hands writers a gift and a trap in the same paper bag. The gift is atmosphere: fog, knocks, costumes, a doorbell after dark. The trap is believing atmosphere is a story.

Here is the clinic's one question for tonight. What does your monster want? Not what it looks like, not what it growls at, but what it came to the door to ask for. A monster that only frightens is a costume. A monster that wants something, and cannot quite get it, is a character.

Try the exercise that writers were passing around this week: peel past the surface fear to the fear underneath it. The spider is rarely the point. The point is what the spider reminds your character of, and what they are afraid is true about themselves.

This is the Method doing its quiet work, dressed up for the holiday: one want, one obstacle, one change on the last line. Cause and effect, not a parade of spooky things happening one after another.

Try it as a labeled demonstration in the Arcade: a writer, a last knock, a polite visitor with a favor to ask. Then close the Arcade, open your draft, and let your own monster ask for something real. Tomorrow, November begins, and the draft will not write itself. Tonight, at least, it can knock.
