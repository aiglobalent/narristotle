You Came Home With a Tote Bag. Now Write One Scene.

Every conference ends the same way: with a tote bag, a notebook full of other people's brilliant advice, and a draft that has not moved an inch. The advice is not the problem. Admiring it is.

THE SHORT ANSWER

The conferences are over and the tote bags are home, so here is the one rule that turns notes into pages: write one scene before you unpack. A scene with a want, an obstacle, and a change on the last line beats a notebook full of admired advice.

Here is the cure, and it fits on a sticky note: write one scene before you unpack. Not a chapter, not a plan, not a reorganized outline. One scene, with a person who wants something in the room and does not get it easily.

If the scene stalls, ask the two questions that never lie. Who wants what, right here, right now? And what is different on the last line than on the first? A scene that can answer both has an engine. A scene that cannot has a diagnosis, which is almost as good.

This is the Method doing its quiet work: dramatic cause and effect, not a parade of things that happen one after another. One thing causes the next, a want meets an obstacle, and the collision changes something. That is the whole job, and it fits in a tote bag much better than a tote bag fits in a novel.

Try it as a labeled demonstration in the Arcade: give your returning writer a souvenir, a mishap, and a promise, then watch the promise do the work. Then close the Arcade, open your draft, and keep the promise yourself. The tote bag can wait by the door. It has waited this long.
