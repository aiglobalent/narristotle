Let the Villain Talk. Then Make Him Pay for It.

Readers are swooning over villain chapters this week, and not because villains are edgy. A villain chapter is the one place a story stops pretending that everyone wants the same thing, and that honesty is catnip.

THE SHORT ANSWER

BookTok's latest obsession is the villain's point of view, and the lesson is not that villains sell. It is that a villain who wants something specific, and pays for wanting it, is the most honest engine in a story.

The trap is handing the microphone to the villain and letting him monologue about his sad childhood until the plot files for unemployment. Sympathy is not structure, and a backstory is not a reason for the reader to turn the page.

The fix is the Method's oldest rule: dramatic cause and effect. Give the villain a want, an obstacle, and a price. Therefore and but, never and then. The villain wants the crown, therefore the hero guards it, but the guard has a debt, therefore the villain buys the debt, and now everybody is in trouble in a way that matters.

Try it as a labeled demonstration in the Arcade: a villain, a prized object, and a betrayal, then watch what changes on the last line. If nothing changes, you have a speech. If something changes, you have a scene.

And if you are starting a November draft on Sunday, let the villain have chapter two. Just make him earn it, and make him pay for it. The readers will forgive him anything except being boring.
