How to Turn Messy Notes Into a Story Outline

Every finished story I have ever admired began as an embarrassment: a phone full of fragments, three contradictory endings, and a note that says only “the bell thing??” Messy notes are not a failed outline. They are unassembled furniture. The job is assembly, and assembly has an order.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Find the person, the problem, and the choices that connect your ideas. Scenes you cannot connect with a choice do not belong in the outline yet—they belong in a drawer, which is a respectable place.

Step 1: Find the person

Read your notes and circle every sentence that is about a someone. One of those someones appears more often, wants more loudly, or embarrasses you most by how much you care about them. That is your protagonist. If three people tie, ask which one has the most to lose. Stories belong to the person with the most at stake, not the person with the best hat.

Step 2: Find the problem

Now circle the sentences where something is wrong, missing, threatened, or about to change. Your protagonist’s problem is the wrong thing that will not politely resolve itself. One sentence is enough: “The lighthouse goes dark in three nights, and she is the only keeper left who knows why.” If your notes hold four problems, pick the one the others make worse. Complications are seasoning; the problem is the meal.

Step 3: Connect the rest with choices

Take what is left — the scenes, images, jokes, and brilliant dialogue with no home — and test each one with a single question: does this happen because of a choice the protagonist made, or does it force a new one? If yes, it earns a line in the outline, placed where that choice belongs. If no, it goes in the drawer. The drawer is not a rejection. Half of my favorite scenes in literature feel like drawer scenes that found their choice later.

Then link the outline lines with two small words: therefore and but. She hides the logbook, therefore the inspector suspects her, but the storm arrives early… If a link only accepts “and then,” you have found a loose board. Either give it a cause or move it to the drawer.

A worked example

Messy notes, as found in the wild: “ferry town / retired captain who won’t go to sea / storm coming / granddaughter arrives with a camera / the old bell from his first ship / something about a debt / great final image: lights on the water.”

Assembly: the person is the captain (most to lose: his pride and his secret). The problem: a debt comes due when the storm grounds the ferry — his only income. The choices: he refuses his granddaughter’s help (therefore she investigates alone), but the bell from his first ship is the collateral (therefore hiding it, lying about it, and finally ringing it become scenes, not decorations). The lights on the water now have something to mean. That is an outline: seven notes in, a story out.

TRY THIS TODAY

Give your current pile twenty minutes and three highlighters: one color for the person, one for the problem, one for anything caused by a choice. Whatever remains uncolored goes to a drawer page — not deleted, drawer. Then write your outline as a chain of therefores and buts, one line per scene.

The takeaway: an outline is not your notes, tidied. It is your notes, causally connected. Person, problem, choices — in that order. The mess was never the obstacle. The mess was the material, waiting for its therefore.
