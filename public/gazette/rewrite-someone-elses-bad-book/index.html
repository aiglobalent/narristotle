Rewrite Someone Else's Bad Book (On Purpose)

THE SHORT ANSWER

A hot thread on r/writing asks whether rewriting a bad book is a useful exercise. It is — not as mockery, but as diagnosis: a book that bores you shows its broken setups, flat extras, and and-then plotting in plain sight.

Over on r/writing today, writers are asking each other a deliciously cheeky question: have you ever rewritten someone else's bad book as a writing exercise, and what was it like? It sounds like mischief. The Gadfly's verdict is that it is actually one of the most honest diagnostics a writer can run, because a book that bores you is not a mystery — it is a patient with symptoms.

Pick the scene that lost you, not the whole novel. Keep the premise and throw out the execution. Then rewrite it with the Method's favorite test in mind, labeled demonstration: does each beat happen therefore or but, or does it merely happen and then, and then, and then? A bad book usually fails that test in public, which is why it is such a generous teacher.

Give every walk-on a secret while you are at it. The waiter who only pours coffee in the original gets a grievance, a debt, and a reason to be in the room that has nothing to do with your hero. You do not have to publish any of this, and you should not — this is a private workout, not a takedown. The point is to learn the repair, not to dunk on the patient.

Then compare the two versions side by side and ask what actually changed. Usually it is not the plot at all. It is a setup that finally pays off, a character who finally wants something specific, or a scene that finally ends because something changed instead of because the page ran out. That is the whole clinic in one exercise.

If you want low-stakes reps before you touch your own manuscript, the Arcade is built for exactly this kind of play — deal a character, hand them a secret they will never tell the protagonist, and write their entrance. Bad books taught you what not to do; your rewrite teaches you what you can. Writing is rewriting, even when the first draft was somebody else's.

The takeaway: Pick the scene that lost you, keep the premise, throw out the execution — and ask whether each beat happens therefore or but, or merely and then, and then, and then. Bad books taught you what not to do; your rewrite teaches you what you can. — See the Method.
