Why “Waiting for the Muse” Is Just Procrastination With Better PR

Somewhere right now, a talented person is not writing because the mood has not arrived. The coffee is right. The playlist is right. The document is open. Nothing is happening — and it feels, somehow, like the noble part of the process.

NARRISTOTLE’S VERDICT

Inspiration helps. A character who wants something—and a consequence for going after it—gives you somewhere to begin.

Let me be clear before the poets write letters: inspiration is real. A line arrives in the shower. A stranger’s argument in a café hands you a whole second act. Those moments are gifts. The problem is not inspiration. The problem is the waiting room we have built around it — the belief that the feeling must arrive before the work, like a visa stamp for a country you already live in.

The muse is a guest, not a landlord

Here is the uncomfortable arithmetic of waiting: a story does not need your mood. It needs a person, a want, and a problem. Mood is weather. Structure is architecture. You would not refuse to build a house because you did not feel like a carpenter today — and if you did, you would at least have the honesty to call it what it is.

Waiting feels virtuous because it resembles reverence. It is not reverence. It is procrastination that has hired a publicist. The publicist is very good. She has a Greek name and a lovely singing voice. Fire her anyway.

What to do instead: a want and a consequence

When the page is blank, do not ask, “What do I feel like writing?” Ask two colder, kinder questions:

  • Who wants something? Not something abstract like happiness. Something countable: the shop, the letter, the apology, the last seat on the boat.
  • What happens if they go after it? A want without a consequence is a shopping list. A want with a consequence is a story, because now every step costs something.

That is the whole ignition system. A character who wants something, and a price attached to wanting it. The muse, who is a notorious show-off, tends to arrive once there is something on the page worth improving.

A worked example

Suppose your notes say: “Woman inherits her estranged father’s failing bakery.” Pleasant. Static. A premise sitting politely in a chair. Now give her a want and a consequence: she wants to sell the bakery by Friday and never think about her father again — but the buyer only wants the building, and selling means firing the two bakers who raised her more than he did.

Notice what just happened. You do not need inspiration to write the next scene. You need to know what she does on Tuesday morning when the bakers arrive early, as they always do, and start the ovens. The decision makes the scene. The scene, once it exists, invites the muse far more reliably than the waiting room ever did.

TRY THIS TODAY

Open your stalled project and write one sentence in this shape: “[Character] wants [specific thing] by [deadline], but getting it will cost [something they value].” Then write the scene where they take the first step anyway. Badly is fine. Badly is a draft. Waiting is not.

The takeaway is not “never be inspired.” It is that inspiration is a terrible project manager and an excellent consultant. Let it consult. Do not let it schedule. Begin with a want and a consequence, follow the trouble they create, and let the muse do what she has always done best: show up late, take the credit, and — occasionally — deserve it.
