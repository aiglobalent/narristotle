November Is a Dare, Not a Deadline

THE SHORT ANSWER

The organization behind the November novel challenge is gone, but the dare survived. Day six is when the word count stops mattering and the scene starts.

Day six. The adrenaline of November 1st has worn off, and the blank page has stopped being romantic. Good. Romance was never the point. The point is the pile: fifty thousand words by the 30th, about 1,667 a day, written by people who decided the dare was worth keeping even after the organization behind it folded last year.

Here is the part nobody prints on the poster. The draft is not the book. It is the quarry. You are not building the house in November; you are cutting the stone, and the writers who finish are the ones who stop polishing individual blocks in week one.

Picture it. A writer in Kauai this weekend, notebook open between conference sessions, stealing twenty minutes to write the scene where her heroine finally says no. That is the whole game, and it fits in a coffee break.

The Method puts it plainly: every scene needs a cause and an effect, a therefore or a but, never a sleepy and then. Give her a reason to walk out the door. Give staying a price she can feel. Then the next sentence has somewhere to go.

Screenwriters get the same dare in a different shape: ninety pages in thirty days, and a script for Molepeople that just outlived its director and its star. A good draft survives the people who were supposed to shepherd it. So write the bad version. Write it fast and ugly, before the voice in your head files its objections. December is for fixing. November is for finishing.

The takeaway: Write the bad version, fast and ugly. December is for fixing. November is for finishing. — See the Method.
