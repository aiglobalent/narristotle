Come Home From the Conference and Write the Scene

Bouchercon, Jackson Hole, and the Florida writers' gathering have all just closed their doors for the year, and the BookFest's virtual weekend is over too. That means a great many writers are home again this week, suitcases half-unpacked, notebooks full, heads buzzing with panels and hallway conversations and other people's excellent advice.

THE SHORT ANSWER

The conferences are over and the notebooks are full, which is exactly when the real trap opens: admiring good advice feels like writing. Notes earn their keep only when they turn into a scene, so unpack the story before you unpack the suitcase.

Here is the kind part of the contrarian warning: a conference is not a novel, and a notebook is not a draft. Advice is wonderful precisely because admiring it feels like working. You can spend a whole happy week reorganizing your notes, color-coding the panels, and telling friends what you learned, while the blank page waits politely for someone braver than a summary.

Try the Method's favorite check, labeled demonstration: read your notes and look for therefore and but, not and then. She attended the panel, therefore she felt inspired, but she had not written a scene. That sentence is honest, and it is also a diagnosis. Notes earn their keep when they produce a scene that surprises you, not when they produce a tidier set of notes.

The practical move is almost insultingly small, which is why it works. Before you unpack, write one imperfect scene drawn from the conference itself: a hero who wants something, an obstacle that says no, and a choice that costs something. Let the panel wisdom show up in the scene, not in the margin.

If the blank page still feels too solemn, borrow the Arcade's approach, labeled demonstration: deal yourself a returning writer, a conference souvenir, and a mishap on the way home, then write the scene where the excuse stops working. Nobody applauds a notebook. Readers applaud a character who finally, messily, begins.

Bring the idea. Even the messy version.

Your notes, your voice, your story. Let’s get to the draft.
