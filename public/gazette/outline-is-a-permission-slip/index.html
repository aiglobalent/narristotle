Your Outline Is a Permission Slip, Not a Novel

Preptober is nearly over, and November 1 is standing in the doorway with its coat already on. The old organization behind the November novel challenge is gone, but the challenge itself refused to die, carried on by successors and by writers who simply liked the dare. That is heartening, and it is also the trap: it is very easy to spend October lovingly building a map of a book you never actually enter.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Preptober ends this week, and the kindest lie writers tell themselves is that one more week of planning is progress. Planning earns its keep only when it gets you to a first imperfect page, otherwise it is procrastination in a nicer outfit.

Here is the contrarian bit, said with love. An outline is a permission slip, not a novel. It tells you where you are allowed to go, and then it sits there, beautifully color-coded, while the blank page waits for someone braver than a plan. If your outline has grown a second outline, or a spreadsheet to manage the first outline, you are not preparing anymore. You are postponing, and postponing has excellent stationery.

Try the Method's favorite check, labeled demonstration: read your plan and look for therefore and but, not and then. She planned, therefore she felt ready, but she had not written a scene. That sentence is honest, and it is also a diagnosis. A plan earns its keep when it produces a scene that surprises you, not when it produces a tidier plan.

The practical move is almost insultingly small, which is why it works. Write one imperfect page before breakfast, before the scroll, before the day has a chance to argue with you. Not a good page. A page. A hero who wants something, an obstacle that says no, and a choice that costs something. Do that for a week and your outline will stop being a permission slip and start being a map of somewhere you have actually been.

If the blank page still feels too solemn, borrow the Arcade's approach, labeled demonstration: deal yourself a reluctant hero, an excuse they have polished to a shine, and a place they hide from their own novel, then write the scene where the excuse stops working. Nobody applauds an outline. Readers applaud a character who finally, messily, begins.

Bring the idea. Even the messy version.
