Purple Prose Is Not the Problem

A professor declared war on the word 'as,' Reddit noticed, and the old purple-prose argument followed right behind. Writers love a rule they can count, but the adjectives were never the problem.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Reddit spent Friday arguing about purple prose and one professor's war on the word 'as.' The real problem was never the adjectives. It was the scene underneath them.

Somewhere on Friday, a professor declared war on the word 'as.' Reddit noticed. The thread pulled in hundreds of upvotes, and right behind it came the other eternal argument: people use the term purple prose way too liberally. Two threads, one shared itch. Writers love a rule they can count.

Here is the trouble with word-counting rules. You can strip every 'as' from a draft and still have a scene where nothing happens. You can dye the prose beige and still bore a reader to sleep by page three. The adjectives were never the crime. The crime is a scene with no turn in it, a character who wants nothing, a paragraph that could be cut without anyone noticing.

Picture it. A writer in Kauai this weekend, notebook open between conference sessions, crossing out 'as' in a paragraph that needs a whole new reason to exist. Or a novelist in Sanibel, where Tara Westover is on the bill this weekend, remembering that her memoir sold on what happened next, not on the polish of any single sentence. The fix is never a smaller word. The fix is a bigger decision.

The Method says it plainly, and it has said it for two thousand years: a scene moves by cause and effect, therefore or but, never a sleepy and then. Give the character a choice with a price on it. Then the sentence takes care of itself. Purple prose is just a loud symptom. Cut the symptom and the patient still limps.

So keep your 'as.' Keep your adjectives, too, if they earn their keep. Day seven of the November dare is a bad day to be timid: the word count is due, the draft is ugly, and the only rule that matters is the one that gets you to the next scene. Write the sentence. Then write a better scene around it.

The takeaway: Write the sentence. Then write a better scene around it — that is the only rule that matters. — See the Method.
