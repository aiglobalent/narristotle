Dialogue Is Negotiation, Not Conversation

NARRISTOTLE’S VERDICT

Real conversation meanders. Good dialogue does not. In a scene, every speaker wants something from the other person, and every line is a move to get it. Find the ask hiding under the small talk, and the dialogue starts writing itself. Miss the ask, and you get two characters exchanging information nobody needed to hear.

Read a flat dialogue scene and you will find the same defect: the characters talk like people at a bus stop — greetings, recaps, polite agreement — and nothing happens.

Nobody Owes Your Backstory a Reading

NARRISTOTLE’S VERDICT

Your backstory is homework, not the story. The reader does not owe your first fifty pages of world history a reading just because you enjoyed writing them. They need, at most, three facts — and only when those facts change what a character does next. Put the rest in your notebook, where it belongs, and smuggle the three that matter into scenes where somebody wants something.

Every writer has a notebook full of history their story doesn't need yet. The question isn't whether the history is fascinating — it's whether it changes what a character does next.

The Two-Question Scene Test

NARRISTOTLE’S VERDICT

Before you write any scene, answer two questions: who wants what in this scene, and what changes by the end of it? If you can't answer both, you don't have a scene yet — you have a location where characters happen to be standing around. Fix the want or the change, and the scene usually fixes itself.

Most dead scenes don't fail because the prose is bad. They fail because nothing is at stake and nothing moves. A character walks into a room, describes the room, has a pleasant conversation, and leaves exactly the person they were when they walked in. The reader can't tell you what the scene was for, and neither can you — because it wasn't for anything.
