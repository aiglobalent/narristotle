Nobody Owes Your Backstory a Reading

Every writer has a notebook full of history their story doesn't need yet. The question isn't whether the history is fascinating — it's whether it changes what a character does next.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Your backstory is homework, not the story. The reader does not owe your first fifty pages of world history a reading just because you enjoyed writing them. They need, at most, three facts — and only when those facts change what a character does next. Put the rest in your notebook, where it belongs, and smuggle the three that matter into scenes where somebody wants something.

The Problem

Every writer has done this. You build a world — or a family, or a career, or a war — and you fall in love with it. You know the founding of the town, the three generations of the feud, the year the bakery burned down and who rebuilt it. You open your story with all of it, because how else will the reader understand anything?

Here is the uncomfortable answer: they won't understand anything yet, and they don't need to. Understanding is not the entry fee to a story. Wanting to know what happens next is.

An opening full of history asks the reader to do schoolwork before they have any reason to care. They have no character to root for, no problem to worry about, no question hanging in the air — just a lecture, delivered by an author who is clearly very proud of the research. The reader's entirely reasonable response is to put the book down and go do something else. Not because they are lazy. Because you have given them nothing to care about yet.

This is where the usual advice goes wrong. "Show, don't tell" doesn't fix it — you can show fifty pages of history in loving scenic detail and it will still be fifty pages of homework. "Start in medias res" doesn't fix it either, if the action stops every few paragraphs so the narrator can explain why it matters. The problem is not how the history is delivered. The problem is that it is delivered before it is needed.

Backstory earns its page space the same way everything else does: by changing a decision, raising a stake, or explaining why this character, right now, cannot simply walk away. A fact that does none of those things is trivia, however fascinating you find it. And trivia belongs in your notebook, not your opening.

The Worked Example: The Bakery Opening That Lectures First

Here is an opening I made up to show you the failure mode. Meet Ana, who has come home to sell her family's bakery. Read the opening the way a stranger would — someone who has never met Ana and has no reason to care about her town:

The bakery had stood on Mill Street since 1962, when Ana's grandmother, Rosa, opened it with money borrowed from her brother, a man who never forgave her for leaving the family farm. Rosa's daughter, Marta, took over in 1989, the year the mill closed and half the town lost its work. Marta married a schoolteacher, and when she died, the bakery passed to her son, who ran it into debt before disappearing. The town council had tried twice to buy the building. The recipe for the almond cake dated to Rosa's village, and no one outside the family had ever made it correctly. Ana arrived on a Tuesday, with a suitcase and a letter from a lawyer, to sell the place.

That is a family saga compressed into a paragraph, and it still reads like a plaque on a wall. Notice what the reader learns: dates, a feud, a mill closure, a disappearance, a council, a cake recipe. Notice what the reader does not learn: what Ana wants, what stands in her way, or why Tuesday matters. She has a suitcase and a lawyer's letter, and we have a history lesson. If you cut the entire paragraph, the story loses information — but it gains nothing, because no scene has started yet.

Now, the repair. The rule is simple: a backstory fact may enter the story only at the moment a character needs it to get something, avoid something, or decide something. So ask the only question that matters about each fact: who needs this, and why now?

Three facts actually matter for Ana's story:

  1. The almond cake recipe is a family secret no outsider has ever made correctly. That matters because someone is about to offer Ana money for it.
  2. The town council has tried twice to buy the building. That matters because the council is about to try a third time, with a deadline.
  3. Ana's brother ran the bakery into debt and disappeared. That matters because the debt is why Ana cannot simply keep the place — and because the brother may not be as gone as everyone thinks.

Everything else — the founding year, the borrowed money, the mill closure, the schoolteacher marriage — goes in the notebook. It informed the writer. It does not need to inform the reader, at least not yet.

The Repair: Three Facts, Smuggled Into Scenes Where Someone Wants Something

Same character, same bakery, same Tuesday. But now the history arrives only when somebody wants something:

Ana arrived on a Tuesday, with a suitcase and a letter from a lawyer. The bakery's ovens were cold. Before she had even set the suitcase down, Mrs. Delgado from across the street was at the door, offering cash — real cash, counted out on the counter — for the almond cake recipe. "Your grandmother never sold it," Ana said. "I'm not my grandmother," Mrs. Delgado said, "and you're not staying." Ana did not sell it. She did not know yet whether that was loyalty or spite, and that not-knowing bothered her more than the cold ovens did.

The council letter was waiting on top of the lawyer's letter. Third offer. Thirty days to accept, or the building would be condemned for the new road. Ana read it twice, then folded it into her pocket, because the second thing waiting for her was a voicemail from a number she did not recognize, in a voice she did — her brother, the one who had run the bakery into debt and vanished, saying he was back in town and wanted to talk about the ovens.

Count the facts. The recipe secret: delivered in a scene where Mrs. Delgado wants something and Ana has to refuse her. The council's interest: delivered as a deadline with teeth, in a letter Ana reads because she has to decide. The brother's debt and disappearance: delivered as a voicemail that changes everything Ana planned to do that week. Three facts, three wants, three decisions. The founding year never appears. The mill closure never appears. The reader misses neither, because the reader was never promised them — the reader was promised a woman, a bakery, and a week that is about to go sideways.

That is the test to run on your own opening. Go through it with a pen and mark every fact that is history rather than action. For each one, ask: does a character need this fact, right now, to get something or decide something? If the honest answer is "the reader needs it to understand the world," cross it out. The reader needs to understand the situation, and a situation is a person who wants something and cannot simply have it. The world can wait. It will still be there in chapter three, when the reader finally cares enough to ask about it.

TRY THIS TODAY

Take the first ten pages of your draft — or the first scene, if you are just starting — and do the backstory audit:

  1. Highlight every sentence that explains the past rather than showing the present. History, family trees, world rules, old wars, how things came to be.
  2. For each highlighted sentence, ask who needs it and why now. If the answer is "the reader, for context," delete it or move it to your notebook. If a character needs it to make a decision in this scene, keep it — and move it to the exact line where the decision happens.
  3. Keep at most three facts in the opening. Not three per page. Three, total, before the story is underway. The rest can enter later, one at a time, each carried in by a character who wants something.
  4. Read the opening aloud without the deleted history. If the scene still works — if Ana still arrives, still refuses, still gets the letter and the voicemail — the history was never load-bearing. It was decoration, and decoration is what revision is for removing.

If this feels ruthless, good. It is supposed to. Your notebook will forgive you. Your reader will thank you — by turning the page, which is the only review that counts.

Backstory is not a debt the reader owes you. It is a setup you plant, and a setup only matters if it pays off — in a choice, a confrontation, or a consequence the reader can feel. That is the Setups & Payoffs pillar doing its quiet work: every fact you smuggle into a scene where somebody wants something is a promise, and every promise kept is a reason to keep reading. Write the whole history if you love it. Then publish only the three facts that change what happens next, and trust the reader to meet you the rest of the way.
