Dialogue Is Negotiation, Not Conversation

Read a flat dialogue scene and you will find the same defect: the characters talk like people at a bus stop — greetings, recaps, polite agreement — and nothing happens.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Real conversation meanders. Good dialogue does not. In a scene, every speaker wants something from the other person, and every line is a move to get it. Find the ask hiding under the small talk, and the dialogue starts writing itself. Miss the ask, and you get two characters exchanging information nobody needed to hear.

The Problem

Read a flat dialogue scene and you will usually find the same defect. The characters talk the way people talk at a bus stop. They greet each other. They recap things they both already know. They agree, politely, and move on.

That is conversation. It is also dead air.

The bad advice here is “make dialogue sound natural.” Natural is the wrong target. A transcript of real talk is mostly filler, false starts, and weather. Readers do not want a transcript. They want a negotiation with the filler stripped out, where each person is trying to win something, dodge something, or keep something hidden.

So stop asking whether a line sounds like something a person would say. Ask what the speaker is trying to get by saying it. A line that sounds perfectly natural and gets nobody anywhere is still a bad line. A line that sounds a little pointed, a little evasive, a little too careful, and moves the negotiation one inch forward is doing its job.

Here is the method. For each speaker in the scene, write down one ask in plain words. Not a theme. Not a feeling. An ask. “I want you to stay.” “I want you to leave without a fight.” “I want you to admit you were wrong, so I do not have to be the one who says it first.” If you cannot write the ask in one sentence, you have not found it yet.

Then read each line of dialogue against that ask. Does the line advance it, block it, disguise it, or deflect it? A line that does none of those four things is small talk wearing a costume. Cut it or give it a job.

The Worked Example: Before

Two sisters, June and Priya, in a kitchen. Their mother has left them a house. June lives three hours away. Priya has been paying the upkeep. This is the version where nobody has an ask:

“Hi,” June said.
“Hi. Coffee?” Priya said.
“Sure. Thanks.”
“So, the house. We need to decide what to do with it.”
“I know. It’s a big decision.”
“It is. Mom loved this house.”
“She did. I miss her.”
“Me too. We should probably talk about the money part at some point.”
“Yeah, at some point.”

Nothing is false here. Every line could really be said. And nothing happens. June and Priya agree with each other five times and leave the kitchen exactly where they started. The reader learns that a house exists and a mother died, both facts the scene could have delivered in a single line of narration.

Now find the asks. June drove three hours because she wants Priya to agree to sell, so she can stop feeling guilty about living far away. She will not say that out loud. Priya wants June to admit that she, Priya, carried the house alone for two years, and she wants that admission before she agrees to anything. She will not say that out loud either.

Two asks. Both unspoken. Now the small talk has somewhere to go.

The Worked Example: After

Same kitchen. Same coffee. Different dialogue, because now each line is a move:

Priya set down a second mug without asking. “You came all this way. You must have something to say.”
June wrapped both hands around the mug. “I was thinking we could talk about the house.”
“Talk,” Priya said. “Or decide?”
“Decide sounds good.” June smiled. “A sale would be clean. No upkeep, no arguments, split down the middle.”
“Clean,” Priya said. “Two years of roofers and plumbers, and you want clean.”
June did not look up. “I didn’t say you didn’t do the work.”
“You didn’t say I did, either.”
A beat. June said, “Then say what you want, Priya. Name it.”
“I want you to say you know what it cost me. Before we talk about a price.”

Look at what changed. Nobody announced their ask. That is the point. June’s “clean” is a bid: let us skip the hard part. Priya’s “roofers and plumbers” is a counter: not before you see what I carried. June’s “I didn’t say you didn’t do the work” is a dodge dressed as a defense. Priya calls the dodge out, and June, cornered, finally asks for the real terms.

Each line advances, blocks, disguises, or deflects an ask. Cut any single line and the negotiation wobbles. That is the test. If a line can vanish without changing what either person wants or gets, it was conversation. If its removal breaks the deal, it was dialogue.

One more thing the repair shows. Subtext is not a fancy extra. It is what happens when two asks cannot be said plainly. June cannot say “absolve me.” Priya cannot say “thank me first.” So they negotiate in code: clean, roofers, price. The code is the scene.

TRY THIS TODAY

Take one dialogue scene from your current draft, the one that reads fine and feels flat. Do this in the margin, before you touch a single line:

  1. Write each speaker’s ask in one plain sentence. If an ask sounds like a theme (“she wants closure”), shrink it until it fits in the room (“she wants her sister to say the words out loud”).
  2. Read the scene line by line. Mark each line: advance, block, disguise, deflect, or none.
  3. Cut every “none” line. Not all of them need deleting; some can be rewritten so they do a job. But none of them get to stay on the grounds that they sound natural.
  4. Rewrite the scene so the asks stay unspoken for as long as you can stand it. Let the reader hear the negotiation before either character names it. The moment a character says the ask outright should feel like a door giving way, not like a summary.

A warning from the other direction. If both characters state their asks in the first four lines, the negotiation ends early and the scene deflates. Keep one ask hidden, or keep one speaker lying about theirs, and the scene keeps its tension. Honesty is a fine virtue. In dialogue, it is often the least interesting move available.

The takeaway: This is the fourth pillar at work: dramatic cause and effect. A line causes the next line. A bid causes a counter. A dodge causes a challenge. “And then she said, and then she said” is conversation. “She pushed, therefore he retreated, but she noticed” is a scene. Find the ask under the small talk, and your characters will stop chatting. They will start negotiating. Readers can tell the difference on the first page. — See the Method.
