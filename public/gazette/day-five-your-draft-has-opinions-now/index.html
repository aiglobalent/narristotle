Day Five: Your Draft Has Opinions Now.

Day five. The honeymoon is over: the first four days ran on adrenaline and a number you could brag about, and day five hands you a chapter that will not behave.

THE SHORT ANSWER

Five days into November, the word count stops being the point. The draft is talking back now, and the fix is one scene where somebody wants something out loud.

Day five. The honeymoon is over. The first four days gave you adrenaline and a number you could brag about. Day five gives you a chapter that will not behave.

Picture it. Your hero finally leaves the house, and the scene lands flat. Not because the sentences are bad. Because she has no reason to go. A character with no want is a mannequin in a nice coat, and no amount of description will make the mannequin walk.

So stop feeding the counter. The count is a blunt instrument. It measures typing, not story. One scene where somebody wants something out loud beats a thousand words of a hero wandering a pretty room.

The Method puts it plainly: cause and effect, therefore and but, never and then. Give her a reason to leave. Give the room a cost for staying. Then the next sentence writes itself, or close enough.

The Arcade has a labeled demonstration for this exact stall: five answers, one stuck scene, one twist. Try it, then close the tab and write the scene where she wants the thing. Fifty thousand words is a pile. A story is a chain. Build the chain.

The takeaway: Fifty thousand words is a pile. A story is a chain. Build the chain — one scene where somebody wants something out loud, and the next sentence writes itself. — See the Method.
