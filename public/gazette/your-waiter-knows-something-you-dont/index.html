THE SHORT ANSWER

Minor characters with private lives read like real people; ones without read like furniture. Today's clinic gives you the thirty-second test — and the three questions your extras must answer before they say a word.

There's a WriterTok short going around this week that revives a very old belief of Steinbeck's: every character should feel like they have a life beyond the page — private hopes, fears, habits, and dreams, even if the reader never sees them. Readers will forgive a simple plot, the argument goes, if they care about the people living through it. They will not forgive cardboard people in the most exciting story ever told.

The clinic's diagnosis is blunter: most manuscripts fail the waiter test. Your protagonist sits down in chapter three, orders coffee, and a man materializes to pour it, recites the specials, and evaporates. He has no past, no grievance, no reason to be there — which is to say, he is a vending machine with a name tag. The reader clocks it instantly, and a small part of the story dies.

The fix takes thirty seconds per character. Before your minor character speaks, answer three questions: What does this person want more than anything? What is their biggest regret? What would they be doing right now if your protagonist had never walked in? You do not need to tell the reader any of it — but you must know it. The knowing is what leaks through the scene and makes them feel real.

This is also where the Method's setups and payoffs quietly do their work. A waiter's offhand complaint about his broken-down truck in chapter three is set dressing; the same complaint, remembered in chapter nineteen when your heroine needs a ride, is craft. Labeled demonstration: the same sentence, doing two jobs.

And if you want to practice on someone else's people first, the Arcade is stocked for it — deal yourself a character card, give them one secret they will never tell the protagonist, then write their entrance. Secrets are cheap; convincing liars are the whole game. Writing is rewriting, and your extras deserve the rewrite too.

The takeaway: Before your minor character speaks, answer three questions: what do they want more than anything, what is their biggest regret, and what would they be doing right now if your protagonist had never walked in? You do not need to tell the reader any of it — but you must know it. — See the Method.
